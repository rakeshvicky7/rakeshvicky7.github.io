An AI Assistant for Structured Fault Diagnosis on Modified Industrial Machinery

M.Sc. thesis · IU International University of Applied Sciences · submission October 2026 In progress

A machine on a factory floor is shaking slightly. Somewhere inside it, something is wearing out. This project is about getting from that shaking to a sentence a technician can act on — check the drive-end bearing first — and being honest about how confident that sentence is.

The question behind it

If a machine has been rebuilt with different parts than it shipped with, what does "normal" even mean for it?

Almost every fault-detection method compares a machine to a baseline. But the machines I saw in a real factory had been modified — different motors, different mountings, parts swapped over years. The vendor's baseline describes a machine that no longer exists. So the question is not only "can a model spot a fault", it is "what is the model comparing against, and who decided that was correct?"

That is the gap the thesis works in. The output is not a label on a screen. It is a procedure: the assistant proposes the most likely cause, tells the technician what to check to confirm or rule it out, and records what the answer turned out to be.

Part one — how shaking becomes numbers

Before any AI, there is a physical question: how does a vibration in metal end up as data in a file? Nothing here is magic — it is a measurement taken very fast, over and over.

The shaft turns. Nothing rotates perfectly. Every bearing, gear tooth and slight imbalance gives the housing a tiny push, thousands of times a minute.

An accelerometer converts the pushes into voltage. Inside it, a small mass presses on a crystal; squeezing that crystal produces a voltage. Push harder, get more voltage. That is the entire trick.

The voltage is read at fixed instants. A computer cannot store a smooth curve, only snapshots. Take them fast enough and the snapshots contain everything you need; take them too slowly and a fast vibration disappears completely, as if it were never there.

Each snapshot is stored as a number. That is the whole dataset — a very long list of numbers with a timestamp. Everything after this point is arithmetic on that list.

In plain words

Think of filming a spinning wheel. If your camera is slow, the spokes look like they are going backwards, or standing still. The wheel did not change — your sampling did. That illusion is a real failure mode in vibration measurement, and it is why the sampling rate is chosen before anything else.

Part two — how a fault hides inside the wave

A recorded vibration looks like noise. It is not. It is several simple repeating movements added together, and a fault is one of those movements arriving at a rhythm it should not have.

This is what a healthy-looking recording looks like. To the eye it is noise, and staring at it tells you nothing.

Any wiggly line can be rebuilt out of simple waves. Slow ones and fast ones, added on top of each other. The maths that pulls them apart is called the Fourier transform, and this is the entire idea behind it — no more than that.

Draw a bar for each rhythm, as tall as it is strong. Now the recording is readable: a tall bar at the shaft's speed, another where the gear teeth meet. These are supposed to be there.

The fault is the bar in the wrong place. A pit in a bearing's outer race gets struck once per pass of a rolling element — a rate you can calculate from the bearing's geometry. Find energy at exactly that rate and you have not guessed the fault; you have measured it.

Why this matters for a modified machine

That last step is the reason this approach survives modification. The expected fault rate comes from the physical geometry of the part actually installed — not from a factory baseline recording of a machine that has since been rebuilt. If someone swapped the bearing, you recalculate the rate; you do not need a new golden reference.

Part three — from a probability to something you can do

A classifier does not output "the bearing is broken". It outputs numbers that sum to one. Turning that into a useful instruction is the part of the problem the assistant exists to solve.

The model's honest output is a spread. 0.61 for a bearing fault is not the same as knowing it. Showing "bearing fault" alone would hide exactly the uncertainty that matters.

So the assistant converts it into checks, in order. Each check is chosen to separate the top candidates as cheaply as possible — the quickest physical test that would make one of them much less likely.

The technician answers from the machine, not from the screen. A person standing next to it has information no sensor captured. The assistant's job is to ask them the right question.

The answer is stored with the reading. Over time this builds the one thing public datasets cannot give you: labelled examples from this specific, modified machine.

What a "model" really is here

It helps to strip the word down. Every recording gets reduced to a handful of measurements — how tall the biggest spike is, how uneven the wave is, how much energy sits at each of the calculated fault rates. Say there are twenty such measurements. That recording is now a single point in a space with twenty directions.

Training means: take thousands of recordings whose fault is known, place them all as points, and find the surfaces that best separate the healthy points from the bearing-fault points from the misalignment points. Predicting means: place the new point and see which side it falls on. There is no understanding in it. It is geometry in a space too large to draw.

Which is exactly why the assistant matters

A point can land near a boundary. The model still returns its most likely answer, with no sense of having been unsure. A structured procedure — propose, check, confirm — is what puts a human judgement back in the place where the geometry ran out.

The datasets

The work is evaluated against three public vibration datasets — MaFaulDa, COMFAULDA and CWRU — where faults were deliberately introduced and the true answer is recorded. They are the only way to measure whether the detection layer is right before it is ever trusted on a real machine.

Where it stands

  • Framing the problem from observed practice in a working factory — done.
  • The detection layer — establishing what can be inferred reliably from raw vibration signals across the three datasets.
  • The assistant — turning a probability spread into an ordered, checkable procedure.
  • Evaluation — testing whether the structured procedure produces more dependable diagnoses than asking the same model directly.
