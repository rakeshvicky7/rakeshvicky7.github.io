Can an AI Agent Diagnose a Machine from Its Own Documents?

M.Sc. thesis · knowledge graph, local LLM, Bayesian diagnosis · prototype on a 3D printer

The thesis asks one question: given only the documents that come with a machine, can an AI agent take a fault described in a technician's own words and lead them to the part that is actually broken? To test it, I built a working prototype for one machine — a Voron 0.2 3D printer — and measured it against real faults. It named the faulty part in its top three for 91% of 69 cases it had never seen, and it runs offline on a laptop.

View the repository

1 · The question

Factories rarely run a machine as it was sold. A base machine is modified, extended, or joined to other units until it fits one production line. The parts are documented by the firms that made them; the modified whole is documented by nobody, and no record exists of how it fails. When it stops, diagnosis falls back on whichever technician remembers this machine, and the sequence that fixed it last time is usually not written down.

Formal methods for this exist and work — fault tree analysis, failure mode and effects analysis — but they take time and training, so with a line stopped nobody uses them. A general chatbot, asked instead, answers confidently and often wrongly: it does not know this machine's parts, its pins, or what its correct values are.

The research question. If an agent is given only the machine's own documents — and that knowledge is structured rather than dumped in as text — can it take a symptom described in everyday words and lead a technician to the part at fault, and to the check that confirms it?

Why a 3D printer is the test machine

Answering that needs one machine where everything required is publicly available: complete documents, a known parts list, firmware configuration — and, crucially, a supply of real faults with confirmed causes to test against. The Voron 0.2, a small open-source printer that owners build themselves from a kit, has all of it, including years of solved cases in public forums.

It also has the property that matters most here: every one is hand-built and then modified, so no two are identical and no manufacturer holds a fault history — the same situation as the modified factory machine, in a form one student can actually get at. Its knowledge is scattered the same way too, across an assembly manual, a parts list, a firmware configuration file and the Klipper firmware documentation.

The constraint was part of the question. Free tools only, fully offline, on an ordinary Windows laptop — the situation a small workshop is actually in, rather than one with a cloud budget and a GPU.

2 · What the prototype does

A small Python server serves three pages. The printer's official CAD model is loaded into all of them, so the machine on screen is the machine in front of you.

The assembly guide

Every assembly of the CAD model laid out in 3D. Assemble builds the printer in the 14 steps of the official manual, in order, explaining at each step what the part does and what it connects to. A chat beside it answers questions like how are the belts connected to the motors? and replays the step that shows it.

The repair assistant

Describe the problem in plain words and it returns the parts to check, in order — each with the failure it suspects, why it suspects it, what to check, and the document that claim came from. Confirming a diagnosis with this was it produces numbered repair steps and stores the case, so the same symptom is recognised faster next time.

Simulation mode

The same diagnosis with the machine drawn in the middle of the screen: settings on the left, chat on the right. It asks for your machine's settings and refuses values outside the allowed range. It then replays the most likely fault on the virtual printer and asks whether that is what yours is doing. The suspect part is highlighted and the rest of the machine dimmed. Answer that the part is fine and it moves to the next suspect; answer that it is faulty and it gives the repair steps.

3 · From a sentence to a part

The core of the system is not the language model. It is a symptom graph built the way an engineer builds an FMEA — a failure mode and effects analysis — read out of the manual, the parts list, the firmware configuration and the Klipper documentation.

Free text, not a form. People describe faults in their own words, so the reader matches keywords, word stems and phrases — and handles negation, so the fan looks normal is evidence against a fault, not for it.

625 everyday wordings sit in front of 106 symptoms. That layer is what lets someone write "rattles" and have the system understand the symptom an engineer would have called excess mechanical play.

Each symptom is linked to the failure modes that can produce it, with a weight for how strongly it points there. The weights are deliberately coarse — 0.9, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3 — because they are expert estimates, and pretending to more precision would be false.

Scores are summed per part. A part suspected by several failure modes at once rises above one suspected strongly by a single mode, which is how a human technician reasons about it too.

Every answer carries its source. The check and the repair steps come back with the document they were taken from, so the technician can verify the claim instead of trusting it.

4 · Where the language model comes in

The graph answers instantly and never hallucinates, but it reasons one part at a time. It cannot notice that three failing parts share a single cable upstream. That is the job given to the model.

Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct runs locally through Ollama. It walks the part-dependency graph looking for a common upstream cause behind the symptoms, and returns its own ranking of suspects, which is fused with the graph's scores — its first, second and third choices carrying weights of 5, 3 and 2.

The order matters for the person using it: the graph's answer appears immediately, and the model's refined ordering arrives a moment later. On a laptop without a GPU that refinement can take up to a minute, which is acceptable only because nobody is made to wait for the first answer.

The virtual printer

The 3D view is the official CAD model converted to GLB and rendered with three.js, with a simulation layer that reproduces the visible effect of each fault and of the machine's settings. Loading it naively cost roughly 2,000 draw calls per frame and crawled on integrated graphics; merging meshes brought that to about 55, which is what makes it usable on the kind of laptop this was built for.

5 · The experiment: which knowledge helps the model most?

Before building the final system, one question had to be answered honestly: does structuring the knowledge actually help, or would feeding the raw documents to a model do just as well? One agent was tested with different knowledge behind it and everything else held fixed — same model, same prompt, same tools, temperature 0, the same 6,000-character evidence budget — across 58 test cases, 30 of them real community cases.

Phase one. “Right part first” is the strict measure; “in top 3” is what matters in practice, because a technician can check three parts quickly.

Structure beats text. Handing the model the same knowledge as searchable documents (B) barely improved on giving it nothing at all (A). Turning that knowledge into a structured part model (C) lifted the top-three rate from 59% to 81%. The result held across three different models — Qwen, Llama 3.1 8B and Mistral 7B — so it is a property of the knowledge, not of one model.

The generic guide made it worse. Arm D — a well-written troubleshooting guide written for 3D printers in general rather than this machine — scored below the model with no knowledge at all: 14% first, against 22%. Advice that is almost right about a different machine is worse than no advice, because it is confident and plausible.

6 · The final result

Phase one's best arms still missed the first guess more often than not, which is what prompted the symptom graph. The final system was developed only on development cases and then run once against 69 held-out cases it had never seen: 30 test cases, 9 real forum cases, and 30 newer real, solved forum cases collected after the system was finished — locked with SHA-256 checksums before that single run, so the result could not be quietly tuned afterwards.

69 held-out cases. “Within one check” means the faulty part was first or second; “within two checks” means it was in the top three. Confidence intervals are 95%.

Read plainly: in about nine cases out of ten, the faulty part is one of the first three the assistant names. The earlier conversational assistant managed 23% first and 69% in the top three on the phase-one cases — a different case set, so the comparison is indicative rather than exact.

The answer to the question

Yes — but only when the knowledge is structured. The same documents handed to the same model as searchable text barely beat giving it nothing. Turned into a part model and then a symptom graph, they took it from 59% to 91% in the top three. What generalises to the next machine is the method — an FMEA-style graph read out of whatever documents exist, scored probabilistically, with a language model used only where graph reasoning runs out. What does not generalise is this graph: it was built by hand for one printer, and the next machine needs its own.

7 · What did not work

Three things were built, measured, and then switched off. They are reported here for the same reason they are reported in the thesis: a result that only lists what worked is not an evaluation.

  • Asking the technician follow-up questions made it worse. Simulated yes/no questioning dropped accuracy from 67% to 50%. A wrong answer early narrows the search in the wrong direction and the system cannot recover.
  • Using the language model to read the symptoms was worse than keywords. Having the model extract symptoms into fixed fields scored 55%, against 63% for the plain keyword reader.
  • A final round of tuning was thrown away. It improved the development cases and hurt the held-out cases — the definition of overfitting — so the earlier, validated version was the one shipped.

8 · What it cannot do

  • It knows one printer model. The method is general; this knowledge graph is not.
  • It was evaluated on written cases, not yet with technicians working on a machine in front of them.
  • The symptom probabilities are expert estimates, not measured failure statistics — there are none for a machine like this.
  • Configuration and firmware faults are sometimes blamed on the mechanical part that shows the symptom rather than the setting that caused it.
  • On a laptop without a GPU the model's refined answer can take up to a minute.

Built with

Python 3.12 with no agent framework · Ollama running Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct · nomic-embed-text embeddings · hybrid BM25 and embedding search fused by reciprocal rank · NumPy · openpyxl · three.js · Docker · Render. A LoRA fine-tune was explored separately on a free Kaggle GPU.
