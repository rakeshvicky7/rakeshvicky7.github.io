Thyroid Disease Detection

Machine learning · clinical time-series · Python, LightGBM, FastAPI

A system that detects an overactive thyroid from the heart rate a smartwatch records while a person sleeps. This page covers the whole build: how the dataset was constructed, how it was prepared, how the model was trained and tested, what it achieves, and what would be required to make it accurate enough for clinical use.

View the repository

1 · The idea behind the project

Thyroid hormone acts directly on the heart. An overactive thyroid — thyrotoxicosis — raises the resting heart rate; an underactive one lowers it. This is not a subtle statistical association but a known physiological effect with a measured size.

A blood test measures thyroid hormone accurately, but only on the day it is taken, and patients are typically tested a month or more apart. A smartwatch measures heart rate every night, continuously, for free. If the hormone effect is large enough to see in that signal, a wearable could flag a developing thyroid problem in the weeks between blood tests.

What the system does

It compares two ten-day windows of sleeping heart rate from the same person — one recorded around a blood test whose result is known, one recorded later — and predicts whether the person has become thyrotoxic by the second window. The output is a probability, a five-state thyroid classification, and the reasons behind the prediction, served over an HTTP API.

The hard part is not the model. It is that no public dataset pairs wearable heart rate with thyroid blood tests, so the dataset had to be constructed — and constructed in a way that does not quietly hand the model the answer.

2 · Building the dataset — the real half

Everything that exists in public data is taken from public data. Only what genuinely does not exist anywhere is generated.

Source data. The Garvan Institute thyroid archive, published through the UCI repository, holds 9,172 real patient encounters — genuine hormone assay panels with diagnoses assigned by clinicians. This is the factual foundation of the project; nothing about the patients is invented.

Filtering. Columns are typed, implausible values removed, and records whose assays were flagged as internally discordant are dropped. 7,484 records survive. Records are discarded rather than repaired, so nothing downstream rests on a guessed value.

Unit mapping. Garvan reports a free thyroxine index; the clinical literature works in free T4 in ng/dL. The two are put on one scale by anchoring their reference intervals — and the result is checked rather than assumed. The mapped values reproduce the expected per-state hormone means in the correct order, to within about 0.1 ng/dL for four of the five states.

State classification. Each record is assigned one of five thyroid states from its free T4 and TSH values. Overt thyrotoxicosis — free T4 above 1.78 ng/dL — is the condition the model has to detect.

Cohort construction. 175 virtual patients are drawn from the pool, stratified so the mix of thyroid states resembles a thyroid outpatient clinic rather than the general-referral population of the source file. Each patient receives three or four visits about a month apart, and one of those visits is a genuine Garvan record held unchanged — the anchor the rest of the patient is built around.

What is simulated, and why only this

The Garvan records are cross-sectional — one panel per encounter. Detecting a change requires the same patient measured twice. Two things therefore had to be generated: the time axis (when visits happen and how hormone levels move between them) and the wearable heart rate, which no public dataset provides alongside thyroid tests.

Both are constrained by the real data rather than invented freely. The relationship between free T4 and TSH is fitted on the real records, not assumed, so simulated visits inherit the true feedback slope and its true scatter. TSH is given a pituitary lag, so it only partly closes the gap to its equilibrium value between one visit and the next. Treated patients relax towards normal, as they do under medication; untreated patients drift. Every simulated value is clipped to the range actually observed in the real cohort.

An approach that was tried and rejected

The first attempt generated the missing heart-rate data with a large language model. That cannot work, for a specific reason: those pipelines produce text instruction pairs, and a language model asked to emit heart-rate numbers returns plausible-looking values with no controlled generative process behind them — no consistent baseline for a given person across visits, no real effect size linking hormone level to heart rate, and no ground truth to recover. A classifier trained on that data learns the language model's habits, not physiology. The approach was abandoned and replaced with a generator built on published physiological constants.

3 · Building the dataset — the simulated heart rate

The generator is written so that every effect in it has a published source and a stated size. Nothing in it is tuned to make the model look good.

Personal parameters. Each simulated person is given a resting heart rate, a personal sensitivity to thyroid hormone, a usual sleep length and a characteristic nightly dip — drawn once and kept for every one of their visits. Without this the data would not be longitudinally coherent, and a change between two windows would reflect nothing but noise between different people.

The hormone effect. The central constant is taken from the clinical literature: about 22 beats per minute per ng/dL of free T4. It is not a tuned parameter, and a unit test asserts that the generator actually reproduces this slope across the clinical range.

Within-night structure. Sleeping heart rate falls through deep sleep and rises during REM. Thyroid hormone blunts that dip and amplifies the REM excursions, so the shape of the distribution changes as well as its average. This is what makes spread, skew and peakedness informative rather than duplicating the mean — and the shape effects are separated out mathematically so they do not inflate the published slope.

Confounders. Three real-world effects are added on purpose. Beta-blockers matter most: they are standard treatment in thyrotoxicosis and they suppress exactly the pathway this system measures, so they are concentrated in the patients it is trying to find. Between-visit baseline drift and intercurrent illness account for the rest.

The output. For every visit the generator produces a ten-day window of nightly sleeping heart rate, complete with the gaps a real device produces when it is not worn. 175 patients yield 619 window–blood-test pairs, which expand to 5,568 modelling cases once every valid pairing of an earlier and a later visit is formed.

A result that was treated as a warning, not a success

The first working version reached a positive predictive value of 93%, far above what this signal achieves on real patients. That was a defect, not an achievement: the generator had made hormone level almost perfectly recoverable from the change in heart rate, so the model was simply inverting the data generator. Adding the three confounders above brought performance down to a realistic level. A synthetic dataset that is too easy produces a model that only works on the synthetic data.

4 · Preprocessing — from two windows to eight numbers

A model cannot take thousands of heart-rate readings as input. Each pair of windows is reduced to eight numbers that describe how the person changed between them.

Two windows, one person. Every case is a pair: a window from a visit whose blood test result is known, and a later window whose thyroid status the model must predict. Pairing within a patient is what turns an absolute measurement into a change.

Four numbers per window. Each ten-day window is reduced to the average heart rate, how much it varies relative to that average, how lopsided the distribution is, and how peaked. Windows shorter than an hour of usable data are rejected outright rather than summarised from too little signal.

The differences carry the signal. Subtracting the referred window's statistics from the target window's removes the person. A resting rate of 78 means nothing on its own; a rise of 17 beats per minute in the same person means a great deal.

Four further inputs. A divergence measure compares the full shape of the two distributions rather than their summary numbers. The known free T4 and TSH from the earlier visit give the model its starting point. The last feature divides the heart-rate change by TSH, because the same rise means more in someone whose TSH was already suppressed.

The model input. Eight numbers per case, and one label: whether free T4 at the target visit exceeded 1.78 ng/dL, the clinical threshold for overt thyrotoxicosis. The features are passed through a quantile transform before training, which puts them all on a common distribution and stops any one scale dominating.

5 · The model — training and testing

A gradient-boosted decision-tree model, tested under two different protocols on purpose.

What goes in. 5,568 cases, each an eight-number description of how one patient changed between two visits. Every feature is first passed through a quantile transform, which reshapes each one onto a common distribution so that no feature dominates because of its units.

How it learns. LightGBM builds a sequence of small decision trees. The first splits the data on whichever feature separates the classes best. Each following tree is fitted to the errors the previous ones made, so the model improves by repeatedly correcting itself rather than by fitting one large rule. Trees were chosen because the relationships here are not straight lines — the effect of a heart-rate rise depends on the TSH level it started from.

The strict test. Patients are split into groups, and no patient ever appears in both the training and the test set. This answers the question that actually matters: how would the system behave on somebody it has never seen?

The literature test. The protocol used in published work on this problem excludes only the specific timepoint being predicted — other visits from the same patient remain in training. That is looser, because the model has already seen that person's body. It is run here anyway, because it is the only way to compare like with like against published numbers.

The gap is the finding. Running both protocols quantifies how much the looser one inflates the result — sensitivity 94.1% against 91.5%. Published work on this problem reports only the looser figure. Reporting both is the more useful engineering result.

The shape of these numbers is what a screening tool should look like. Negative predictive value is very high — when the system says nothing is wrong, it is almost always right — while positive predictive value is moderate, meaning a positive result is a reason to order a blood test rather than a diagnosis. Ten missed cases out of 118 is the number that would need to fall before any clinical use.

What these numbers do and do not mean

The heart-rate half of this dataset is simulated, so these figures describe how the pipeline behaves, not how the idea performs on real patients. The operating point was also deliberately calibrated to a realistic level rather than allowed to run free. A model trained on simulated data can only recover the relationships that were put into the simulator — that limit does not go away with more validation, and only real recordings can remove it.

6 · What would make it clinically accurate

The pipeline is built so that each of these is a substitution, not a rewrite.

Replace the simulated heart rate with real recordings

This is the only change that turns the result into clinical evidence. The system needs a table of patient, visit, date, free T4 and TSH, plus one heart-rate window per visit. Everything downstream — features, model, both validation protocols, the API — stays exactly as it is. Until that data exists, every figure above measures engineering, not medicine.

Validate prospectively, on a different site

Current performance is measured on patients drawn from one archive and calibrated to a known operating point. Genuine accuracy means recruiting patients, recording them forward in time, and reporting whatever comes out — including on a population, era and assay platform different from the one the model was developed on.

Give the model the confounders as inputs

Beta-blockers are currently simulated as an effect the model has to see through. In practice the prescription is known, and so are age, weight change, illness and sleep disruption. Supplying them as features would let the model account for a suppressed heart rate instead of being misled by it — the single largest practical gain available.

Shorten the observation window

The ablation over window length is one of the more useful results here: a five-day window scored better than the ten-day one on every measure — sensitivity 94.9% against 91.5%, specificity 87.4% against 83.8%. A shorter window also means faster detection and fewer nights lost to non-wear. Ten days was retained only because it matches the published specification.

Collect more borderline cases

Specificity falls from 88.8% to 83.8% when subclinical patients are included, and those patients are under-represented here. They are also exactly the group a monitoring tool should be most useful for, since they are the ones a single blood test is most likely to miss. More of them, and a model trained to separate them rather than to treat them as noise, is the clearest route to fewer false alarms.

Set the threshold with clinicians, not statistics

The current cut-off balances the two error types mathematically. In practice the choice depends on what follows a positive result. If it triggers a blood test, a lower threshold and more false alarms is the right trade. If it triggers a change in medication, it is not. That decision belongs to the clinical context and is exposed as a configurable parameter.

Scope

Academic project. Not a diagnostic device, not clinically validated, and not intended to inform any medical decision.
