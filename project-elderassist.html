ElderAssist

Full-stack product · React 19, TypeScript, Supabase, PostgreSQL

A local task marketplace for the elderly parents of people who moved away. A family posts a bounded, paid job — collect the medicines, wait for the electrician, sit with him through the scan — and a nearby helper picks it up. Built as a complete product: two roles, a full task lifecycle, live updates, and rules enforced in the database rather than in the browser.

Open the live app View the repository

1 · The idea behind the project

Migration has separated a generation of Indian parents from their children. Around 35 million Indians live outside the country, and more than a third of the population has moved to a metropolitan city. The parents mostly stayed. What stayed with them are the ordinary jobs a family used to absorb without noticing: the fan that stopped working, the medicines that need collecting, the form that must be submitted in person, the hospital appointment nobody should attend alone.

The usual answer is relatives and neighbours — unreliable, and it costs a favour every time. The organised platforms each solve one standardised transaction: one sends a cleaner, one brings food, one delivers medicine. None of them will collect a document from a government counter, wait in the house while an electrician works, or sit with someone through a scan.

The demand side. Real, recurring, and small. A handful of ordinary jobs a month, in hundreds of different towns, currently absorbed by whoever happens to be nearby.

The supply side. In the same towns, people who want exactly this kind of work: short, local, flexible, paid on completion. That category of work barely exists in an organised form.

Why a company cannot simply hire carers. This is the economic argument the whole product rests on. Demand this sporadic and this dispersed cannot support salaries — paying people to wait would cost far more than any family would pay. So the answer has to be a marketplace, not an agency.

The product is the layer between them. A family posts a task with a title, category, location, budget and optional photograph. A nearby helper sees it, accepts it, and works it through to completion. The aim is narrow and defensible: turn an unreliable informal arrangement into a recorded, observable one, and direct paid work to people in the same locality.

What it deliberately is not

NotBecause
A care agency with employed staffDemand is too sporadic and too dispersed to pay salaries against.
A subscription serviceYou pay for a task when it is done. No plan, no monthly fee, no joining fee, on either side.
A clinical or nursing serviceHelpers collect medicines and accompany people. They do not administer medication or give medical opinions.
A background-check companyHelpers verify a government photo ID. Third-party criminal-record checks are the top roadmap item, and the product says so rather than implying otherwise.

Being explicit about what a product does not do is part of the design. A platform touching vulnerable people should not let a user infer a guarantee it has not made.

2 · Two roles, one lifecycle, and a race condition

Roles are assigned at registration and are structurally distinct — separate dashboards, separate navigation, separate permitted pages. A family member creates elder profiles, posts tasks, follows them to completion, raises emergency alerts and rates the helper afterwards. A helper browses open tasks, filters and sorts them, accepts one and moves it through to completion.

Tasks run open → accepted → en-route → in-progress → completed, with cancelled available from the start. Every transition is timestamped and notifies the other party without a refresh. And exactly one helper may be assigned — which is harder than it sounds.

The situation. A task is open, and two helpers are looking at it. Both tap Accept within the same second. Checking "is it still open?" and then writing would not help — the gap between the two operations is exactly where the collision happens.

Both requests really do arrive. Neither client knows about the other. There is no application server in the middle to queue them.

The condition is part of the write. The update says: set this task to accepted by me, but only if it is still open. The database applies that atomically, so the first request through finds the condition true and takes the row.

The second request matches nothing. By the time it is applied the status is no longer open, so no row is updated. Rather than treating an empty result as success, the code detects it and returns a message the helper can act on. One line of query, one class of bug removed.

3 · Where the rules are enforced

The architecture is three-tier and client-centric, with no bespoke application server: a React single-page app talks over HTTPS and a websocket to Supabase, which fronts PostgreSQL. That choice has one consequence that shapes everything else.

The consequence

With nothing between the browser and the data, client-side validation is advisory only. Anyone can open developer tools and send whatever request they like. So any rule that must genuinely hold has to live in the database, where it cannot be bypassed.

What that looks like in practice

  • Row-level security on every table, keyed on the authenticated user's ID. A family member can read and write only their own rows. A helper can see a task only while it is open, or once it has been assigned to them — the filtering is not done in the app, it is done before the rows are ever sent.
  • Check constraints on status, priority and rating ranges, so an invalid value cannot be stored even by a hand-crafted request.
  • A trigger on signup creates the matching profile row automatically, so an authenticated user without a profile cannot exist.
  • A trigger on ratings writes the new average back to the helper's aggregate, so the displayed score is always derived from the reviews rather than maintained by the client.

Six tables

TableHolds
profilesOne row per authenticated user — name, phone, city, role, languages, verification status, aggregate rating
eldersElder reference records owned by a family member: blood group, conditions, medication, mobility, languages, preferred hospital, emergency contacts
tasksPosted tasks — poster, assignee, lifecycle timestamps, evidence, rating
emergency_alertsUrgent broadcast requests, active or resolved
notificationsMessages addressed to one user, optionally linked to a task
wallet_transactionsBalance top-ups and settlements

Every data access in the app goes through one module that returns { data, error }, so a rejected write surfaces instead of failing silently. In the earlier prototype write results were discarded, which meant a request blocked by a security policy looked identical to one that succeeded.

4 · How the other person finds out

The product only works if a helper learns about a task without opening the app to check. That single requirement drives the whole realtime design.

The connection exists before anything happens. When a helper opens the app it does not only download a list — it opens a websocket and subscribes to the tables it cares about. That line stays open, idle, costing almost nothing.

The family member posts a task. The app sends one instruction: insert this row. It does not notify anybody, because it has no idea who is listening.

The database writes the row. This is the only fact in the system. Every screen is a view of it, which is why two people cannot see different states for long.

The write itself is the announcement. The tasks table belongs to a publication, so Postgres emits the change and the realtime service forwards it to every client subscribed to that table. Nobody asked — the server spoke first, because the line was already there. Notifications and emergency alerts work the same way, which is what fixed the prototype's silent alerts.

The client re-renders one list. The message arrives as a plain object, goes into React state, and only the row that differs is repainted. The card appears while the phone is in a pocket.

Why not just poll

Asking the server every few seconds is simpler to write, and almost every answer is "nothing new". The cost scales with how many people are waiting rather than with how much actually happens — and for a product where most minutes are quiet, that is the wrong way round. A subscription costs one idle connection and delivers exactly one message, at the moment there is something to say.

5 · A demo anyone can open

A marketplace is hard to show. It needs two accounts, a database, environment variables and someone willing to register before they have seen anything. So the build produces a second artefact: one self-contained HTML file holding the entire product — marketing site, family app and helper app, with all CSS and JavaScript inlined. No server, no database, no configuration. Open it, email it, or host it anywhere.

One codebase. Pages, components, routing and the design system are shared and untouched. The demo is additive — it does not fork the product or maintain a second version of anything.

Two build configurations. The demo build substitutes the Supabase client for an in-memory stand-in implementing the same query-builder, auth, storage and realtime surface, backed by fixture data. Writes really mutate that store, so accepting a task removes it from the pool and rating a helper changes their average. Nothing survives a reload, which is the point.

The output. A single HTML file with everything inlined. It uses hash-based routing, because a static file is served by a host that knows nothing about the app's routes, and a small switcher — present only in this build — moves between the marketing site and the two role dashboards without inventing credentials.

6 · What changed, and what is still open

This is the second version. The first was a university software-engineering project; rebuilding it meant listing what was actually wrong rather than adding features on top.

Prototype limitationNow
Emergency alerts were not liveAdded to the realtime publication and subscribed
Notifications never reached helpers; read state never writtenLive channel, mark-as-read, sent to both parties on every transition
Write results discarded — rejected writes failed silentlyAll data access returns { data, error }; failures surface
Elder profiles could not be edited or deletedFull create, read, update and delete
No addressable URLsReal routing; every view is bookmarkable and shareable
Fixed-width sidebar, no mobile layoutResponsive shell with a mobile drawer
No ratingsRatings, reviews, and a trigger-maintained helper aggregate

Still open, and stated rather than hidden

  • The task-image storage bucket is still public. It should be private with signed URLs.
  • There is no automated test suite. Unit tests and end-to-end tests against a seeded project are the next piece of work.
  • Disputes have no representation in the system. There is no administrative role, and a marketplace handling money eventually needs one.

Decisions worth defending

The interface uses a 17-pixel base size, one step larger than a typical product, because a share of the people involved are over sixty. Motion is driven by a single observer and disabled entirely under the reduced-motion system setting. Chart colours were checked against a colour-vision-deficiency validator — lightness band, chroma floor, adjacent-pair separation and contrast all pass — and the repository notes that a fourth categorical hue must not be added without re-running that check.
